How You Can Help at Home

Supporting your child at home can make a real difference. Here are some practical strategies:

Build Confidence

  • Celebrate effort, not just correct answers
  • Avoid time pressure for calculations
  • Emphasize that everyone learns differently
  • Share stories of successful people who struggled with maths

Use Concrete Materials

  • Use real objects (toys, coins, buttons) for counting and calculations
  • Play board games that involve counting and number recognition
  • Bake together - measuring, fractions, time
  • Use money in real shopping situations

Practice Little and Often

  • 10-15 minutes daily is better than one long session
  • Make it fun and low-pressure
  • Use games and apps designed for dyscalculia
  • Focus on understanding, not speed

Support Number Sense

  • Estimate quantities (how many sweets in the jar?)
  • Compare sizes (which is bigger?)
  • Count in different ways (forwards, backwards, in 2s, 5s, 10s)
  • Use number lines for visual support

Reduce Anxiety

  • Never say “I was bad at maths too” - this reinforces negativity
  • Avoid showing your own anxiety about mathematics
  • Praise perseverance and problem-solving strategies
  • Remind them that struggle is part of learning

Work with School

  • Attend meetings and share observations
  • Ask questions if you don’t understand something
  • Implement strategies consistently at home
  • Communicate what works and what doesn’t

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Will my child grow out of dyscalculia?
A: Dyscalculia is a lifelong condition, but with appropriate support, individuals can develop effective strategies and achieve their potential. Early identification and intervention make a significant difference.

Q: Does dyscalculia mean my child has low intelligence?
A: No. Dyscalculia is a specific difficulty with numbers and is not related to general intelligence. Many children with dyscalculia have average or above-average intelligence.

Q: Can my child still study STEM subjects?
A: Yes, with appropriate support and adjustments. Many successful scientists, engineers, and mathematicians have dyscalculia. Understanding concepts is possible even if calculation remains challenging.

Q: Will my child get extra time in exams?
A: If dyscalculia significantly impacts access to assessments, reasonable adjustments such as extra time, use of calculators, or alternative recording methods may be appropriate.

Q: Is there a cure for dyscalculia?
A: There is no “cure,” but evidence-based interventions can help children develop mathematical skills and strategies to manage difficulties effectively.

Q: Should I hire a private tutor?
A: A tutor trained in dyscalculia-friendly approaches can be beneficial. However, ensure they understand dyscalculia and use multi-sensory, concrete methods rather than just more practice of traditional methods.
